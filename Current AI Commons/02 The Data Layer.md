# Stage 1 — The Data Layer

> _The model is only as good as the data it learned from. This stage is where most real AI product work actually lives._

---

## 🗺️ What we'll cover

The data layer has 5 major phases in sequence:

1. **Sourcing** — where does data come from?
2. **Collection & Ingestion** — how do you get it into your pipeline?
3. **Curation & Cleaning** — how do you make it good?
4. **Formatting & Tokenization** — how does text become numbers the model sees?
5. **Dataset Management** — versioning, lineage, mixing

Let's go through each:

---

## 📡 1. Data Sourcing

Every model starts with a question: _where does the training data come from?_

### **Web Crawls**

The largest pretraining datasets are dominated by web text. **Common Crawl** is the canonical source — a free, petabyte-scale crawl of the web updated monthly. GPT-3, LLaMA, Mistral — all trained heavily on Common Crawl derivatives.

The problem: Common Crawl is _messy_. Raw web HTML includes boilerplate, ads, nav menus, duplicate pages, spam, and low-quality content. Raw CC is never used directly — it's always filtered heavily (more on that in curation).

Other web sources layered on top:

- **Books** (Books3, Project Gutenberg) — long-form coherent text, great for reasoning
- **Wikipedia** — high quality, structured, encyclopedic
- **Code** (GitHub) — critical for coding capability, also improves reasoning generally
- **ArXiv, PubMed** — scientific reasoning
- **StackExchange, Reddit** — conversational, QA format

### **Proprietary / Licensed Data**

At the frontier, labs pay for licensed data. OpenAI has deals with news publishers. Google has all of Google's internal data. This is a genuine moat — hard to replicate.

### **Synthetic Data**

**Increasingly the most important source for post-training.** Instead of paying humans, you use a powerful model to _generate_ training data.

Examples:

- **Alpaca** (Stanford): used GPT-3 to generate 52K instruction-following examples
- **Phi series** (Microsoft): trained small models almost entirely on synthetic "textbook quality" data generated by GPT-4 — achieved remarkable performance at tiny scale
- **Self-instruct, Evol-Instruct**: iteratively generate harder and more diverse instructions

The risk: **model collapse** — if you train on model-generated data recursively, quality degrades over generations. Active research area.

### **Human-Labeled Data**

Expensive but irreplaceable for certain things — especially **preference data** (used in RLHF). You need humans to say "response A is better than response B." Scale AI, Surge AI, Remotasks are the dominant vendors. Frontier labs have internal red teams generating adversarial data too.

## 🔄 2. Collection & Ingestion

Once you know your sources, you need pipelines to get data in at scale.

### **Web Crawling**

If you're not using Common Crawl, you run your own crawler. Tools: **Scrapy**, **Playwright** (for JS-heavy sites). At scale this becomes a distributed systems problem — crawl frontiers, politeness policies, dedup of URLs.

### **Data Formats**

Raw data comes in many shapes:

- **JSONL** — the standard for LLM training data. One JSON object per line. Each object typically has a `text` field (pretraining) or `instruction`/`response` fields (fine-tuning).
- **Parquet** — columnar format, efficient for large-scale data processing with Spark/DuckDB
- **WebDataset (tar shards)** — used for multimodal data (image-text pairs), efficient streaming during training

### **Streaming vs. Batch**

At pretraining scale (terabytes), you can't load everything into memory. Training pipelines stream data directly from object storage (S3/GCS) using **streaming dataloaders** — shuffling happens at the shard level, not globally.

## 🧹 3. Curation & Cleaning

This is where the real craft is. **Data quality >>> data quantity** at post-training scale, and even at pretraining scale quality filtering dramatically improves results.

### **Deduplication**

The web has massive redundancy — the same article republished 1000 times. Training on duplicates wastes compute and causes models to memorize rather than generalize.

Two levels:

- **Exact dedup**: hash-based (MD5/SHA on document or n-gram level). Fast, cheap.
- **Fuzzy dedup**: MinHash + LSH (Locality Sensitive Hashing) — finds near-duplicates. Used by LLaMA's data pipeline. More expensive but catches paraphrased duplicates.

At scale, **suffix arrays** over the full corpus can find repeated n-grams — used by DeepMind's MassiveText pipeline.

### **Quality Filtering**

Several signals used to filter low-quality documents:

|Filter|What it catches|
|---|---|
|**Language ID**|Non-target language content|
|**Perplexity filter**|Text scored by a small reference LM — very high perplexity = likely garbage|
|**Heuristic rules**|Too short, too many special chars, too many repeated lines, bad word/char ratio|
|**ML classifier**|Train a fastText/BERT classifier on "high quality" vs "low quality" examples|
|**NSFW / toxicity filter**|Hate speech, explicit content — using classifiers like Perspective API|

**CCNet** and **C4** (Google) are examples of filtered Common Crawl derivatives. **FineWeb** (HuggingFace, 2024) is currently the best open filtered web dataset.

### **PII Scrubbing**

Remove personally identifiable information — emails, phone numbers, SSNs — using regex + NER models. Critical for privacy compliance.

### **Data Mixing**

You don't train on one source. You mix them at carefully chosen ratios. LLaMA 2's mix was roughly: web (67%), code (8%), books (7%), Wikipedia (4%), etc.

The ratios matter enormously — **upsampling** high-quality sources (books, Wikipedia, code) beyond their natural frequency improves performance disproportionately. Getting these ratios right is an empirical art.

## 🔢 4. Formatting & Tokenization

This is where raw text becomes the actual numbers the model trains on.

### **Tokenization**

Models don't see characters or words — they see **tokens**. A tokenizer converts text into a sequence of integer IDs from a fixed vocabulary.

**BPE (Byte Pair Encoding)** is the dominant algorithm:

1. Start with individual bytes/characters as vocabulary
2. Repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token
3. Repeat until vocabulary size is reached (typically 32K–128K tokens)

Examples of how tokens look:

```
"Hello world"  → [9906, 1917]
"tokenization" → [30085, 2065]  (might split into sub-words)
"GPT"          → [38, 2898, 51]  (or a single token if frequent enough)
```

**Why it matters for products:**

- Tokenization determines **context window cost** — longer tokenizations = more tokens = more expensive
- Languages tokenize very differently — English is efficient, Chinese/Japanese/Korean tokenize much less efficiently in English-trained tokenizers → worse performance per token
- Code tokenizes differently than prose — indentation, symbols, variable names all have implications

**Tokenizers in use:**

- **tiktoken** (OpenAI) — used by GPT-4, very fast
- **SentencePiece** — used by LLaMA, T5, multilingual models
- **HuggingFace tokenizers** — wraps most of the above

### **Chat Formatting / Prompt Templates**

For instruction-tuned models, raw text isn't enough. You need a structured format that tells the model who is speaking. Every model family has its own template:

**LLaMA 3 format:**

```
<|begin_of_text|>
<|start_header_id|>system<|end_header_id|>
You are a helpful assistant.
<|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>
What is the capital of France?
<|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
```

**Why this matters:** If you fine-tune a model, you must use the _exact_ chat template it was trained with. Wrong template = garbage outputs.

### **Packing & Sequence Length**

Training efficiency demands you fill every context window completely. **Packing** concatenates multiple short documents into a single training sequence up to the max context length, separated by EOS tokens. Without packing, short documents waste compute on padding.

## 🗄️ 5. Dataset Management

### **Versioning**

Code has Git. Data needs versioning too. **DVC (Data Version Control)** is the standard — it tracks large files in cloud storage while storing metadata in Git. You get reproducibility: "this model was trained on dataset commit abc123."

HuggingFace **datasets** library also handles versioning + streaming + caching natively and is the de facto standard for open datasets.

### **Data Lineage**

In production you need to answer: _where did this training example come from? Was it contaminated? Did it appear in the eval set?_ Lineage tracking means logging source URLs, processing pipeline versions, and filter decisions for every document.

**Contamination** is a serious problem — if your eval benchmark's answers leaked into training data, your benchmark scores are meaningless. Dedup between train and eval is a required step.

### **The Eval Dataset**

Separate from training data — held out completely. Typically:

- **Validation set** — used during training to monitor loss, detect overfitting
- **Test set** — touched only at final evaluation, never during training
- **Benchmark suites** — MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K, HellaSwag etc. — standard public benchmarks used to compare models

## 🏗️ How it all fits together — The Full Pipeline

```
Raw Sources
(Common Crawl, GitHub, Books, Synthetic)
        ↓
   Ingestion
(crawl, download, format as JSONL/Parquet)
        ↓
   Curation
(dedup, quality filter, PII scrub, language filter)
        ↓
   Mixing
(sample each source at target ratios)
        ↓
   Tokenization
(BPE → integer token IDs)
        ↓
   Packing
(fill context windows, add EOS separators)
        ↓
Training-ready shards
(streamed from object storage into GPU training run)
```

## 💡 Developer Relevance Callouts

- **For fine-tuning:** You'll spend most of your time in formatting + quality filtering. A few hundred high-quality JSONL examples beats thousands of noisy ones.
- **For RAG:** Chunking strategy _is_ your data layer. Chunk size, overlap, metadata tagging — these decisions dominate retrieval quality more than model choice.
- **For evals:** Contamination checking between your fine-tune data and your eval set is non-negotiable.
- **Tokenizer awareness:** Always check how your data tokenizes. A "10K document dataset" might be 2M tokens or 20M tokens depending on content — and that changes your fine-tuning compute budget entirely.
